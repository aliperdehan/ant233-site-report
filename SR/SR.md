## Introduction

The Krapina rockshelter is widely known for the Neanderthal remains found there.
The two prevalent camps around the site are those who consider the broken bones as the leftovers of cannibalism and those who regard them as the remnants of careful burial.
This report argues that this framing is too narrow, as what the assemblage can show about behaviour is dependent on how it formed and how it was recovered.
It summarises the history, setting, chronology and preservation of the site, describes the materials recovered, and evaluates what they reveal about technology, mobility, mortuary treatment and symbolic behaviour, before discussing the importance of Krapina.

## Site History and Location

Krapina is a collapsed rockshelter in a sandstone bluff above the Krapinica River in the Hrvatsko Zagorje region, about 40 km NNW of Zagreb.

The Palaeolithic deposits were excavated between 1899 and 1905 by the Croatian palaeontologist Dragutin Gorjanović-Kramberger, who recorded roughly 11 m of Pleistocene sediment in nine major levels and published the finds in two monographs, in 1906 and 1913 [@simek1997, pp. 561--562].
The recorded thickness differs between accounts: 11 m in @simek1997 [p. 562] and @karavanic2004 [p. 187], over 9 m in @miracle2011 [p. 88].

Krapina is the first Palaeolithic site discovered in Croatia [@karavanic2004, p. 185].
Nothing remains of the site except the eroded sandstone cliff face [@radovcic2015, p. 2].

<!-- map -->

Regionally, Krapina belongs to a continental zone in northwestern Croatia where Middle Palaeolithic groups occupied caves, such as Vindija, Velika Pećina and Veternica, whereas the milder Dalmatian coast, separated from the northwest by the Dinaric Mountains, yields mostly open-air sites [@karavanic2004, pp. 185--186].
Beaver remains come mostly from the fluvial base of the shelter (layer 1), which is consistent with a river-margin setting [@radovcic2015, p. 2].

## Stratigraphy, Chronology, and Climate

The cultural layers (levels 2 to 9) contain sublevels and lenses, which indicate a more complex depositional history than the nine-level scheme implies.
Most hominin fossils come from levels 3 and 4, the so-called Hominid Zone, whilst the richest lithic concentrations occur in levels 3 and 8 [@simek1997, pp. 562, 565].
A sterile rockfall about 1 m thick overlies level 4, which suggests that the shelter wall was already collapsing during the occupation [@trinkaus1985, p. 204].

Gorjanović-Kramberger proposed that the faunal remains indicate that the deposits were formed during a warm period, whilst the later research extended this history to the last glacial [@simek1997, p. 562].
The upper levels show cold and cold-temperate macrofaunas, and on this evidence Malez placed layers 2--4 at the "terminal last interglacial or initial last glacial", and the subsequent levels in the early last glacial [@trinkaus1985, p. 204].
Electron spin resonance (ESR) dating of tooth enamel, with mass spectrometric U-series analysis used to evaluate the uranium-uptake history, gives a mean of 130 ± 10 ka for levels 1--8, which puts the fluvial sequence in the last interglacial (MIS 5e).
A single sample from level 9 gives an ESR average of 87 ± 7 ka and a U-series average of 113 ± 10 ka [@rink1995].

Workmen excavated the shelter with shovels, bones were labelled by level instead of being recorded in position, and dynamite was used to remove large blocks, leaving fresh twentieth-century breaks on many specimens [@trinkaus1985, pp. 204–205].
<!-- horrible -->
The sediment pressure, rockfall, the activity of cave bears and rodents and hearths built next to buried remains could have broken or scattered the bones [@trinkaus1985, pp. 206–209].
The scarcity of other modifications on cave bear bones suggests that sediment covered them quickly [@miracle2011, p. 107].

## Materials Recovered

The lithic assemblage comprises 1,191 artefacts, of which 273 fit Bordes' Middle Palaeolithic tool types and 918 are classed as waste.
38 retouched pieces among those are listed outside the type list [@simek1997, pp. 565, 567].
It is a scraper-rich Charentian Mousterian, in which sidescrapers make up more than half of the tools, notched and denticulated pieces and naturally backed knives are common, and Levallois blanks are present but not abundant [p. 566].

The fauna is dominated by large mammals.
Among 25 mammalian taxa, rhinoceros (21.7% of the minimum number of individuals, MNI), bears (17.0% of MNI) and bison or aurochs (7.5% of MNI) are the main large mammals, whilst beaver, which derives mostly from the fluvial base of the shelter, is given separately as 17.9% of the bones [@radovcic2015, p. 2].
@miracle2011 [p. 86] nevertheless describes the cave bear as the numerically most important taxon, so the ranking may depend on the counting unit.
Burning and stone tool cut marks occur on part of the mammalian fauna (13.6% and 4.1%, respectively) [@radovcic2015, p. 2].

The hominin sample is the best known component, with more than 850 skeletal remains (about 900 in Simek and Smith's count), principally from levels 3 and 4 [@rink1995; @simek1997, p. 563], although it remains primarily a collection of isolated and incomplete bones [@trinkaus1985, p. 203].

The later metric and qualitative work, however, found it consistent with Neanderthal variation [@simek1997, p. 564].

## Technology, Subsistence and Mobility

@simek1997 [pp. 561--562] describe a technology as built on local river cobbles.
The cobbles were opened as cobble wedges, producing flakes with remnant cortex and many naturally backed knives.
A few large artefacts of exotic stone, for which no waste occurs on site, indicate that some materials were curated as groups moved across the landscape [p. 562].
Typology remains stable, as sidescrapers vary between 50% and over 90% of the tools in each level without any systematic trend.
Raw material choice and blank production, though, do change, namely, volcanic tuff dominates the early levels (and also levels 5 and 9), silicified tuffs and cherts become more important later, and Levallois flaking is more frequent in the lower than in the upper unit [pp. 567, 569--570, 572].
Silicified tuff forms only ~1% of the modern river gravels but is abundant in the hominin-rich levels 3 and 8, meaning it was either selected at the river or brought in from further afield [p. 569].
The authors read these shifts as behavioural change among Neanderthals, not as replacement by modern humans, but the trends remain tentative, since no level reaches the 100-tool minimum for typological analysis, and only retouched tools were assigned to levels [pp. 561, 565--566].

The Krapina Neanderthals also "appear to have been successful hunters of large game, including Merck's rhinoceros" [p. 188].

@miracle2011 [p. 89] reports little evidence of hominin involvement in the cave bear remains: 2.2% of the bones carry cut marks, 1.1% are gnawed, and over 75% are whole.
He concludes that the last major phase of occupation, in level 9, "[was not by Neandertals but by] ... prime-adult to old male cave bears" [pp. 86, 107], but collection and curation biases have had a major impact on the assemblage [p. 87], and only 12 bear bones carry level information (layers 1, 5--6, 7, 9) [p. 88].
The uppermost layers (8/9), which Gorjanović-Kramberger called the "Ursus spelaeus zone", also hold at least one hearth, a few Mousterian tools and the talons described above [@radovcic2015, pp. 3, 11], so they may well record use by both bears and Neanderthals, which goes against his stated reading.
The two need not conflict, though.
A hearth and a few tools do not necessarily constitute a major phase of occupation, the zone spans layers 8 and 9 rather than level 9 alone, and Miracle himself raises the time-shared use of caves by bears and hominins [p. 86].

## Mortuary Treatment and Symbolic Behaviour

Gorjanović-Kramberger proposed cannibalism in 1906, and @trinkaus1985 [pp. 204--205] reviewed the fragmentation, cut marks, burnt bone and disassociation of the skeletons on which the proposal rests.
None of these patterns can be explained solely by cannibalism [p. 203], and the preservation of skeletal parts indicates that the Neanderthals were buried, by natural or human processes, soon after death [pp. 203, 205--209], although the data are insufficient to say whether the burial was accidental or intentional [p. 213].

The evidence for symbolic behaviour rests on a much smaller sample.
@radovcic2015 [pp. 1, 3, 10] report that four of the eight talons and the phalanx carry cut marks, mostly with smoothed edges, that all eight talons show polished facets or abrasion, and that three of the largest have similar notches, which they read as the result of mounting the talons in a necklace or bracelet.
Other Mousterian sites, such as Pech de l'Azé and Fumane, have yielded only isolated talons [p. 2].
This reading depends on interpreting the smoothing as wrapping in fibre, which the authors say they "assume" [p. 11] without experimental or chemical work [p. 10], and they cannot rule out taphonomic causes for the notches [p. 10].
The talons are also assigned to a single level on thin evidence [pp. 3, 10--11], so the symbolism is inferred instead of observed.

## Relevance to the Eurasian Record

Krapina holds what @trinkaus1985 [p. 203] described as "the largest sample of archaic human remains known from one site".
<!-- the situation may have changed -->
The restriction of its sequence to MIS 5e makes it a reference point for the last interglacial in Central and Southeastern Europe [@miracle2011, p. 107].

The talons, in the reading of @radovcic2015 [p. 1], indicate ornament production well before modern humans appeared in Europe.
@simek1997 [pp. 561, 564], performing the lithic reanalysis, interpret the change seen in the stone tools as behavioural change among Neanderthals, so a transitional population, as @rink1995 raised for level 8, need not be invoked to explain it.

According to @saladie2017 [pp. 1042, 1062], Krapina is perhaps the most controversial of the Neanderthal assemblages linked to cannibalism between ~130 and 40 ka, which makes it a methodological test case.

Krapina also anchors a regional sequence.
The Vindija Neanderthals, whom @karavanic2004 [p. 189] reports as directly dated by AMS to 29 and 28 ka, lived in the same area some 100 ka after those of Krapina.

## Conclusion

The evidence suggests that the Neanderthal population of Krapina utilised local stone, curated a few exotic pieces, hunted large game, processed its dead, and modified eagle talons.
Each of these inferences, however, is limited by the passage of the evidence through rockfall, carnivores and early excavation.
The trends in lithics are tentative, the bear and talon levels are based on thin evidence, and the cut marks on the human bones support opposing readings without being able to definitively distinguish between cannibalism and secondary mortuary practices.
Both interpretations of the broken bones are thus dependent on material whose formation is only partially known.
In future work, a single cut-mark protocol could be applied to hominin, ungulate and eagle bones from the same levels, such as the criteria that @radovcic2015 [pp. 2, 8] applied in part to the talons.
